\section{Discussion}
The results support a qualified answer to the three research questions. Student
developers report frequent use of Generative AI across research, code
generation, debugging, documentation, and related activities, but the
perception families do not form a single longitudinal trajectory. Repository
activity and clean source/test change concentrate near the final evaluation
anchor, while candidate coordination-language density increases in the
observed transcript corpus. Finally, the study finds observable variation in
upfront architectural-artifact scope, but no robust monotonic association with
late clean-path rework or independent blind-scored outcomes. The central
implication is therefore not that a larger artifact necessarily produces a
better project, but that AI-assisted development makes the quality and
visibility of shared intent consequential while leaving its effects difficult
to identify with small observational samples.

\subsection{From local acceleration to system-level coordination}

RQ1 and RQ2 describe two sides of AI-assisted development. Survey responses
show that students use Generative AI for concrete development activities, while
Repository Activity (M3) and Clean-Change Dynamics (M4) show that
repository-visible activity intensifies around final integration.
Coordination-Language Evidence (M5) adds textual evidence that candidate
coordination markers are
more dense at later checkpoints in the observed 2025.2 corpus. These results do
not establish that AI use caused coordination friction; the relevant
measurement boundaries are consolidated in Threats to Validity. They do,
however, weaken a simple productivity story in which faster local code
production automatically yields smoother system integration.

The more defensible interpretation is a shift in the bottleneck. Generative AI
can reduce the effort required to produce local implementation fragments, but
it does not remove the need to agree on architecture, interfaces, task
boundaries, dependencies, or acceptance conditions. In a multi-person project,
those agreements remain coordination objects even when code is produced more
quickly. The late concentration observed in Repository Activity (M3) and
Clean-Change Dynamics (M4) is consistent with a process
in which integration pressure accumulates until the system must be assembled
under deadline, while Coordination-Language Evidence (M5) suggests that
coordination-related language is already
present in the shared record. This is a process interpretation, not a causal
estimate.

The robustness analyses refine that interpretation by showing that the late
peak should not be collapsed into a single ``student syndrome'' explanation.
Some team-semesters combine high final-seven-day concentration with a positive
T3--T1 score delta, which is consistent with compensatory late execution or
productive recovery under deadline pressure. Others combine similarly high
late concentration with no evaluator-score gain, which is more consistent
with delayed integration that did not translate into assessed improvement.
Still others improve with lower final concentration, indicating that
distributed execution remains a plausible path to strong outcomes. The point
is not that one profile is the true mechanism, but that repository timing,
planning visibility, and evaluator trajectories separate several mechanisms
that a simple procrastination narrative would conflate.

\subsection{Architectural artifacts: necessary evidence, not a quality score}

RQ3 provides an important qualification to strong planning narratives.
Architectural Artifact Scope (M6a)
measures whether an architectural artifact was observed in the repository and
how much structural scope it occupied; Planning Content Evidence (M6b) extracts
literal, structured planning-content evidence from T1 commit subjects. Neither
metric directly
measures architectural quality. Repository absence cannot establish that a team
did not plan elsewhere, and a larger artifact cannot establish that its design
was coherent or useful.

The M9 associations are therefore modest and unstable
rather than uniformly directional. With only 14 team-semesters, RQ3 has low
power for confirmatory correlation, and its coefficients serve only as
exploratory diagnostics of structural variation. The paper's stronger
empirical contribution lies in the descriptive and triangulated RQ1--RQ2
evidence collected across the approximately 70 participating students, while
the survey, repository, and transcript streams retain their distinct grains
and coverage. T1 artifact scope has small positive
associations with T3 rework magnitude in the complete team-semester set, while
the baseline-eligible rework-ratio associations are near zero and slightly
negative. Associations with blind-scored outcomes are likewise mixed and small.
The leave-one-out ranges show that individual team-semesters can materially
change the observed coefficient. The result is not evidence against planning;
it is evidence against treating repository-visible artifact volume as a
validated measure of planning quality.

The complexity profiles add a second qualification. Late clean-path rework can
reflect technical difficulty and project structure as well as planning gaps or
AI-assisted implementation dynamics. The stratified summaries show that some
associations change direction across lower- and higher-complexity strata, so
complexity is best treated as a concurrent explanation that bounds the
planning interpretation. This does not make complexity a causal adjustment in
the present design; it makes explicit why rework should not be attributed to a
single source.

The distinction also matters for interpretation of the tail cases. A team with
no observed T1 artifact and a team with an artifact that is incomplete,
misaligned, or unused are not equivalent cases. Conversely, a high rework ratio
does not by itself identify a defect: Clean-Path Rework (M8) follows clean-path provenance and
cannot determine whether a change was semantically destructive. The empirical
chain is consequently useful as an auditable observation system, but not as a
single planning-quality scale.

\subsection{Why Spec-Driven Development is a plausible response}

The results provide a strong rationale for discussing Spec-Driven Development
(SDD). The argument is not that this study tested SDD, nor that SDD is proven
to reduce rework. Rather, the observed combination of rapid local generation,
multiple late-execution profiles, repeated architectural reformulation in the
project records, technical-complexity sensitivity, and persistent coordination
demands identifies precisely the class of problem that SDD is designed to
address: keeping intent, constraints, architecture, and tasks explicit before
and during implementation.

Current SDD guidance describes a workflow in which a specification is carried
through technical planning, task decomposition, implementation, and explicit
convergence checks~\cite{github2025spec,thoughtworks202Xspec}. This is aligned
with the mechanism suggested by the present evidence. If Generative AI makes
implementation cheap while leaving system-level decisions underspecified, a
process that externalizes those decisions can reduce ambiguity before it is
multiplied across generated code and parallel contributors. The potential
benefit is not documentation for its own sake; it is a shared, inspectable
context for coordinating people and AI tools.

The claim must remain conditional. The current data do not compare SDD with
another process, do not randomize teams or interventions, and do not measure
specification adoption as a treatment. SDD is therefore a theoretically
grounded intervention proposal for future evaluation, not a causal conclusion
of this study. A suitable follow-up would compare teams using an explicit
specification-to-plan-to-task workflow with teams using their usual process,
while measuring architectural change, integration timing, rework provenance,
coordination evidence, and independently scored outcomes.

\subsection{Implications for Agile practice}

These findings do not imply that iterative development should be abandoned.
They suggest that Agile iteration under Generative AI requires a clearer object
of iteration. When code can be generated rapidly, iteration should include
shared architectural decisions, executable acceptance conditions, interface
contracts, and explicit dependency boundaries, rather than treating working
code as the only visible progress signal. This interpretation is compatible
with Agile values while challenging a purely code-first implementation rhythm.
It also separates adaptive iteration from unexamined delay: late work can be
compensatory, neutral, or harmful depending on whether it is connected to
shared intent, integration checks, and assessed improvement.
Granting teams autonomy to use Scrum, Kanban, other Agile variants, or an
unguided approach was a deliberate feature of the naturalistic course design,
preserving the process choices students ordinarily make rather than imposing a
common treatment. This choice prevents us from attributing the observed T3
friction specifically to Generative AI or to any one process model; instead, it
motivates controlled tests of explicit architectural checkpoints, including
SDD-inspired rubrics, within otherwise iterative workflows.

From a pedagogical perspective, the observed coordination bottleneck is
ambiguous rather than automatically beneficial. Integration friction may
create productive opportunities for students to reason about architecture,
interfaces, and dependencies, but it may also reveal only near the deadline
that rapid AI-assisted implementation had concealed gaps in that
understanding. The present instruments measure activity, perceptions,
coordination-language evidence, artifacts, rework proxies, and project
outcomes; they do not directly measure individual architectural learning.
Accordingly, future classroom studies should pair these process measures with
pre/post concept assessments and structured student reflections that can
distinguish productive struggle from delayed recognition of foundational
gaps.

The practical implication is a stronger planning layer, not a return to a
fixed plan that cannot change. Specifications should remain revisable, but
their revisions should be explicit, reviewable, and connected to the tasks and
implementation they govern. For educators evaluating early SDD artifacts,
simple heuristics such as file scope (M6a) are insufficient. Instead, rubrics
should assess both shared architectural intent and students' reasoning through
explicit API contracts, defined cross-component dependencies, justification
of design revisions, and verifiable acceptance criteria before GenAI-assisted
implementation begins. That is the specific space in which SDD deserves to be
tested: as a way to preserve adaptability while making the intent behind
AI-assisted change inspectable and the associated learning visible.

\subsection{Scope of the interpretation}

The Discussion is intentionally bounded by the evidence design. RQ1 uses
repeated cohort summaries rather than a stable individual panel; the remaining
coverage, window, proxy, sample, and exploratory-association boundaries are
consolidated in Threats to Validity. These limits make the findings useful for
mechanism-oriented hypothesis generation, but not for estimating the causal
effect of Generative AI, architectural planning, or SDD on project outcomes.
The strongest conclusion is narrower: under
AI-assisted development, local generation speed does not eliminate the need for
explicit system-level coordination, and SDD is a plausible, testable response
to that coordination problem.
