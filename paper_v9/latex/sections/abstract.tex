\begin{abstract}
Generative AI can make local software implementation faster, but it may also
raise the importance of shared architectural intent, integration, and human
coordination. This study examines that tension across two cohorts of student
software projects comprising 14 team-semesters and three synchronized
checkpoints. We combine multi-checkpoint surveys, repository mining,
independent blind-scored evaluator scorecards, and transcript-based evidence to
study Generative AI adoption and role perceptions, the temporal density of
repository activity, and associations between upfront architectural artifacts,
late rework, and project outcomes. The results show that self-reported AI use
is widespread and task-oriented, but perception families do not form a single
longitudinal trajectory. Repository activity and clean source/test change
concentrate near final integration, while candidate coordination-language
density increases across the observed transcript checkpoints. Observable T1
architectural-artifact scope varies substantially, yet its associations with
T3 clean-rework magnitude, baseline-conditioned rework ratios, and independent
outcomes are small, mixed, and sensitive to individual team-semesters. These
findings suggest that Agile remains necessary, but AI-assisted development
requires a more explicit and inspectable layer of architectural planning,
interfaces, dependencies, and acceptance conditions. Spec-Driven Development
is discussed as a strong, testable response to this coordination pressure, not
as an intervention validated by the present observational study.
\end{abstract}
