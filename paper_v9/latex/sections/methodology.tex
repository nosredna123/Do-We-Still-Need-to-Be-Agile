\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

To investigate how planning discipline and coordination evolve in AI-assisted
software engineering projects, we designed an empirical study combining
longitudinal repository mining, standardized instructor evaluations,
multi-checkpoint student surveys, and qualitative meeting transcripts across
two academic offerings.

\subsection{Context, Course Design, and ``Rules of the Game''}
The study was conducted within a project-based undergraduate software engineering course at a large public university in South America, spanning two semesters: 2025.2 ($N = 9$ analyzable teams) and 2026.1 ($N = 5$ teams), yielding a total inferential dataset of $N = 14$ team-semesters ($\approx 70$ participating students).

Students formed teams of approximately five members through spontaneous self-allocation, with remaining students assigned to vacant slots to ensure balanced team sizes. Teams enjoyed complete autonomy regarding their internal organization, process models, and project management methodologies (e.g., choosing freely between Agile variants, Scrum, Kanban, or unguided approaches). However, course guidelines enforced two structural constraints:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Teams were required to develop an operational software project featuring an integrated Generative AI component as a core functional requirement, but no particular AI architecture was mandated. Course instruction presented Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)---which grounds Large Language Models (LLMs) in external knowledge bases \cite{lewis2020retrieval}---as one domain-dependent example rather than a universal requirement. Teams retained autonomy over their architectures, and none of the 14 projects implemented a full RAG pipeline. Instead, they developed project-specific integrations including LLM-supported summarization and content generation, profile or document analysis and matching, conversational tutoring and chatbots, and personalized recommendations, as summarized in Table~\ref{tab:projects}.
	\item Students were strongly encouraged to utilize generative AI assistants throughout all phases of software development, from requirements specification to implementation and testing.
\end{enumerate}

The project lifecycle was anchored by three synchronized temporal checkpoints ($T_1$, $T_2$, and $T_3$) paced identically across both cohorts:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{$T_1$ (Baseline):} Administered on the first day of class prior to any formal instruction on generative AI processes, capturing baseline perceptions, initial project scope, and early planning commits.
	\item \textbf{$T_2$ (Mid-Semester):} Conducted following structured instructional modules covering generative AI mechanics, RAG architectures, and software process models, accompanied by the second wave of survey instruments and status presentations.
	\item \textbf{$T_3$ (Final Integration):} Positioned one week prior to the final project delivery and presentation, capturing the peak pressure of system integration and deadline convergence.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Project Scope and Real-World Complexity}
To ensure ecological validity and induce realistic coordination friction, the course guidelines explicitly prohibited trivial algorithmic exercises and toy applications. Under the course rules, teams were required to identify and solve real-world community, educational, or business problems. This constraint forced students to grapple with high domain complexity, authentic user requirements, and the nontrivial challenge of embedding Generative AI into functional architectures.

The resulting software systems spanned diverse domains, ranging from healthcare and financial management to academic research and ecotourism. Table~\ref{tab:projects} details the 14 projects analyzed in this study. The breadth and complexity of these applications validate that the observed late-stage integration challenges and chronic coordination friction were driven by authentic software engineering workloads rather than artificially simplistic academic tasks.

\subsection{Data Sources and Collection Framework}
\label{sec:data-sources}
Our data collection framework triangulates four distinct evidentiary channels to capture both technical artifacts and human behavioral dynamics.

\textbf{1. Git Repositories:} Complete historical commit logs, branch structures, line change counts, and file-level provenance were extracted from all team repositories across the entire semester lifecycle. Students were explicitly instructed to centralize all project artifacts---including requirements, architectural specifications, documentation, and source code---directly within their Git repositories. This directive defined the repository as the intended source of truth for the team's production effort. The analysis used read-only repository histories and standardized event tables; Git remains repository evidence, not AI-use telemetry.

\textbf{2. Independent Evaluator Scorecards:} At each checkpoint ($T_1$, $T_2$, $T_3$), teams delivered formal status presentations. These were assessed by a panel of independent supervisors (4 evaluators in the 2025.2 cohort, and 3 in the 2026.1 cohort). To prevent groupthink and ensure scoring validity, an independent blind-scoring protocol was enforced: following the presentation and open feedback, each supervisor submitted their scores via a private electronic form. Evaluators remained blind to their peers' scores throughout the semester. The final project score was calculated as an unweighted average of these independent assessments. Projects were assessed across four structured criteria on explicit bounded scales: \emph{Project Evolution} (0 to 4.0 points), \emph{Scope and Applicability} (0 to 2.0 points), \emph{Technical Complexity} (0 to 2.0 points), and \emph{Engagement and Participation} (0 to 2.0 points). These observations were organized by team-semester and checkpoint for analysis.

\textbf{3. Multi-Checkpoint Student Surveys:} Students completed identical survey instruments at $T_1$, $T_2$, and $T_3$ (totaling six instrument cycles). These surveys systematically captured data across five core constructs: Demographics, Professional Experience, GenAI Adoption, Perceptions of AI Impact on SE Roles, and Project Expectations. To ensure replicability, the core dimensions and sample variables tracked across these checkpoints are detailed in Table~\ref{tab:survey_instrument}. Survey responses are cohort-by-semester/checkpoint data and carry no team identifier; Self-Reported AI Usage and Perceptions (M1) and Perceived Role Disruption (M2) therefore remain at that grain.

\textbf{4. Qualitative Meeting Transcripts:} To contextualize coordination dynamics and evaluative feedback, we constructed a bounded, single-cohort qualitative corpus from audio recordings of the three formal status presentation checkpoints in 2025.2. The corpus comprises the full audio records of the student presentations alongside the subsequent deliberative discussions among the supervisors in a private chat group. Crucially, these supervisory discussions were free-form qualitative reflections, completely decoupled from the numeric scorecasting process. To make the transcription data computationally feasible for analysis, the raw text was segmented into analytical chunks, yielding 100 distinct fragments (35 at $T_1$, 37 at $T_2$, and 28 at $T_3$). Coordination-Language Evidence (M5) reconstructs one global corpus per checkpoint; chunks and source sessions are provenance units, not independent observations. This qualitative deep dive informs interpretation of the repository evidence collected across both cohorts, but it is not used to claim cross-cohort replication: no transcript-based comparison with 2026.1 is made, and no transcript evidence is imputed for that cohort.

\textbf{Data Anonymization and Privacy:} To ensure strict ethical compliance and protect participant confidentiality, all raw data streams were subjected to a rigorous anonymization pipeline, combining deterministic heuristics with LLM-assisted redaction to scrub unstructured text across commit messages, survey free-text responses, and audio transcripts. Finalized analytical artifacts exclude direct personal identifiers. Detailed call records and raw payload provenance remain restricted to the internal reproducibility record.

\subsection{Statistical and Analytical Concepts}

The analysis uses descriptive summaries to preserve the scale and grain of each
evidence stream. Means are reported with standard deviations when the metric is
an approximately interval-like score; medians and interquartile ranges are used
for skewed counts and ratios. Proportions retain their denominators, and rolling
seven-day windows are indexed relative to evaluator anchors; overlapping windows
are therefore summaries of nearby periods, not independent observations.

For exploratory associations, Spearman's rank correlation $\rho$ measures the
strength and direction of a monotonic relationship between two ranked variables
without assuming a linear scale~\cite{spearman1904proof}. Its handling of ties
is retained in the metric definition; study-specific limits from sample size,
missingness, and influential observations are addressed in Threats to Validity.
Artifact--Rework--Outcome Associations (M9) reports exploratory p-values as
descriptive diagnostics rather than
confirmatory significance tests. Leave-one-out analysis
recomputes an association after removing each eligible team-semester in turn;
the resulting range shows sensitivity to influential cases and is not a
confidence interval. These summaries are always interpreted with their
denominator, stratum, and measurement-availability state.

\subsection{Advanced Metrics Operationalization}
\label{sec:advanced-metrics}

Sections~\ref{sec:method}A--D established what was collected. This subsection
formalizes how repository history, evaluator scorecards, survey responses, and
transcript text become nine metric families used for RQ1--RQ3. Each family
retains its analytical grain and exposes components only when they represent
distinct constructs; no component is silently combined into a new score.
To improve readability, we retain the M1--M9 identifiers for traceability but
refer to each family by a concise descriptive name throughout the results and
discussion (for example, Self-Reported AI Usage and Perceptions (M1) and
Repository Activity (M3)).

\textbf{A note on $T_1$/$T_2$/$T_3$ for repository-derived measures.} The
checkpoint labels for commit and file history denote windows bounded by each
team's evaluator presentation dates, not single calendar days. This distinction
is retained throughout the repository-derived families and is made explicit in
the corresponding artifacts and provenance comments.

\subsubsection{RQ1: How do student developers utilize Generative AI, and how does this adoption shape their longitudinal perceptions of software engineering roles and project expectations?}

RQ1 is operationalized at cohort-by-semester/checkpoint grain because the
anonymized survey responses contain no team identifier. Self-Reported AI Usage
and Perceptions (M1) separates the
perception panel from self-reported usage frequency, tasks, tools, prior
experience, autonomy/tool balance, and exploratory career topics. Perceived
Role Disruption (M2) preserves
role-specific Likert distributions and paired sensitivity summaries. These
families describe self-report and perceived role disruption; neither is AI-use
telemetry or objective extinction risk.

\textbf{Self-Reported AI Usage and Perceptions (M1).} The six perception
families remain separate. Descriptive distributions are reported alongside raw
usage fields and missingness; no heterogeneous family average is interpreted as
a behavioral dependency measure. Career-topic coding remains exploratory.

\textbf{Perceived Role Disruption (M2).} Role-specific Likert responses are
summarized by cohort, checkpoint, and role. Where privacy-preserving respondent
identifiers permit it, paired T1--T3 sensitivity summaries are reported without
exposing identities.

\subsubsection{RQ2: How does the temporal density of repository activity contrast with the qualitative typification of human coordination friction across the project lifecycle?}

\textbf{Repository Activity (M3).} This family separates phase activity, author
concentration, and rolling seven-day activity. A secondary pooled relative-window
view removes the semester dimension only for visualization; it does not replace
semester-stratified observations.

\textbf{Clean-Change Dynamics (M4).} This family separates clean-change magnitude,
touching-commit intensity, artifact composition, and rolling clean churn. The
consistent path-classification rule defines the included source and test paths;
commit counts remain contextual rather than the primary Clean-Change Dynamics
(M4) outcome.

\textbf{Coordination-Language Evidence (M5).} This family reconstructs one global transcript
corpus per checkpoint and deterministically counts versioned lexical markers for
alignment, handoff, integration, blockers, and rework. It reports density,
composition, corpus coverage, and an auditable chunk queue.
Coordination-Language Evidence (M5) is not joined or correlated with
team-semester Repository Activity (M3) or Clean-Change Dynamics (M4)
observations.

\subsubsection{RQ3: How does the quality of upfront architectural artifacts (evaluated at baseline \texorpdfstring{$T_1$}{T1}) associate with late-stage destructive rework and independent, blind-scored project outcomes?}

\textbf{Upfront Architecture Evidence (M6).} Architectural Artifact Scope (M6a)
reports T1 artifact presence and repository-visible scope. Approved Planning
Content Evidence (M6b) reports separate categories---goals,
architecture/design, task decomposition,
risk/dependency, and literal evidence quotes---for nine observed team-semesters;
five cases without T1 commit subjects were not measured. Planning Content
Evidence (M6b) is exploratory
and is never reduced
to a composite planning-quality score.

\textbf{Repository Inactivity (M7).} This family reports recent seven-day repository
inactivity around evaluator checkpoints and in T3-relative rolling windows. It
describes repository timing and coverage only; it does not identify planning
omission and is not an independent predictor in Artifact--Rework--Outcome
Associations (M9).

\textbf{Clean-Path Rework (M8).} This family separates clean
rework magnitude, the clean rework ratio, the late-stage rolling trajectory,
and baseline eligibility. This is a path-provenance proxy for destructive
rework, not semantic defect validation. A path first observed at T1 or T2 is
eligible for the provenance
analysis; baseline-ineligible cases remain separate and are not imputed as
stability or low quality.

\textbf{M9: associations among artifacts, rework, and outcomes.} This family reports
descriptive Spearman associations between T1
architectural-artifact predictors and Clean-Path Rework (M8a/M8b) or
independent, blind-scored T3
evaluator outcomes, restricting
ratio analyses to the baseline-eligible stratum. Approved
Planning Content Evidence (M6b) fields enter a separate non-composite
association output for observed cases.
Leave-one-out diagnostics expose influence. No Repository Inactivity (M7)
predictor or floor-imputation analysis is used.

\subsection{LLM Usage in Data Extraction, Processing, and Metrics}
Language-model assistance is used selectively for data extraction and is kept
separate from deterministic metric construction. Self-Reported AI Usage and
Perceptions (M1) uses coded survey responses, and Coordination-Language Evidence
(M5) uses deterministic lexical coding of the transcript corpus; neither metric
receives a new language-model score. Planning Content Evidence (M6b) uses structured extraction from
$T_1$ commit subjects for nine observed team-semesters; five team-semesters have
no $T_1$ subjects and remain unavailable rather than being imputed.
