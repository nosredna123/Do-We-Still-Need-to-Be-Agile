\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

To investigate how planning discipline and coordination evolve in AI-assisted
software engineering projects, we designed an empirical study combining
longitudinal repository mining, standardized instructor evaluations,
multi-checkpoint student surveys, and qualitative meeting transcripts across
two academic offerings.

\subsection{Context, Course Design, and ``Rules of the Game''}
The study was conducted within a project-based undergraduate software engineering course at the State University of Ceará (UECE), spanning two semesters: 2025.2 ($N = 9$ analyzable teams) and 2026.1 ($N = 5$ teams), yielding a total inferential dataset of $N = 14$ team-semesters ($\approx 70$ participating students).

Students formed teams of approximately five members through spontaneous self-allocation, with remaining students assigned to vacant slots to ensure balanced team sizes. Teams enjoyed complete autonomy regarding their internal organization, process models, and project management methodologies (e.g., choosing freely between Agile variants, Scrum, Kanban, or unguided approaches). However, course guidelines enforced two structural constraints:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Teams were required to develop an operational software project featuring an integrated Generative AI component as a core functional requirement. Specifically, projects were expected to implement advanced architectural patterns such as Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)---a technique that dynamically grounds Large Language Models (LLMs) in external knowledge bases to mitigate hallucination \cite{lewis2020retrieval}---or LLM-driven data analytics pipelines.
	\item Students were strongly encouraged to utilize generative AI assistants throughout all phases of software development, from requirements specification to implementation and testing.
\end{enumerate}

The project lifecycle was anchored by three synchronized temporal checkpoints ($T_1$, $T_2$, and $T_3$) paced identically across both cohorts:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{$T_1$ (Baseline):} Administered on the first day of class prior to any formal instruction on generative AI processes, capturing baseline perceptions, initial project scope, and early planning commits.
	\item \textbf{$T_2$ (Mid-Semester):} Conducted following structured instructional modules covering generative AI mechanics, RAG architectures, and software process models, accompanied by the second wave of survey instruments and status presentations.
	\item \textbf{$T_3$ (Final Integration):} Positioned one week prior to the final project delivery and presentation, capturing the peak pressure of system integration and deadline convergence.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Project Scope and Real-World Complexity}
To ensure ecological validity and induce realistic coordination friction, the course guidelines explicitly prohibited trivial algorithmic exercises or ``toy'' applications. As part of the ``rules of the game,'' teams were mandated to identify and solve real-world community, educational, or business problems. This constraint forced students to grapple with high domain complexity, authentic user requirements, and the nontrivial challenge of embedding Generative AI into functional architectures.

The resulting software systems spanned diverse domains, ranging from healthcare and financial management to academic research and ecotourism. Table~\ref{tab:projects} details the 14 projects analyzed in this study. \subsection{Data Sources and Collection Framework}
Our data collection framework triangulates four distinct evidentiary channels to capture both technical artifacts and human behavioral dynamics.

\textbf{1. Git Repositories:} Complete historical commit logs, branch structures, line change counts, and file-level provenance were extracted from all team repositories across the entire semester lifecycle. To mitigate the threat of unobserved out-of-band collaboration, students were explicitly instructed to centralize all project artifacts---including requirements, architectural specifications, documentation, and source code---directly within their Git repositories. This directive ensured the repository served as a comprehensive, single source of truth for the team's entire production effort. The analysis used read-only repository histories and standardized event tables; Git remains repository evidence, not AI-use telemetry.

\textbf{2. Independent Evaluator Scorecards:} At each checkpoint ($T_1$, $T_2$, $T_3$), teams delivered formal status presentations. These were assessed by a panel of independent supervisors (4 evaluators in the 2025.2 cohort, and 3 in the 2026.1 cohort). To prevent groupthink and ensure scoring validity, an independent blind-scoring protocol was enforced: following the presentation and open feedback, each supervisor submitted their scores via a private electronic form. Evaluators remained blind to their peers' scores throughout the semester. The final project score was calculated as an unweighted average of these independent assessments. Projects were assessed across four structured criteria on explicit bounded scales: \emph{Project Evolution} (0 to 4.0 points), \emph{Scope and Applicability} (0 to 2.0 points), \emph{Technical Complexity} (0 to 2.0 points), and \emph{Engagement and Participation} (0 to 2.0 points). These observations were organized by team-semester and checkpoint for analysis.

\textbf{3. Multi-Checkpoint Student Surveys:} Students completed identical survey instruments at $T_1$, $T_2$, and $T_3$ (totaling six instrument cycles). These surveys systematically captured data across five core constructs: Demographics, Professional Experience, GenAI Adoption, Perceptions of AI Impact on SE Roles, and Project Expectations. To ensure replicability, the core dimensions and sample variables tracked across these checkpoints are detailed in Table~\ref{tab:survey_instrument}. Survey responses are cohort-by-semester/checkpoint data and carry no team identifier; M1 and M2 therefore remain at that grain.

\textbf{4. Qualitative Meeting Transcripts:} To capture coordination dynamics and evaluative feedback, audio recordings were collected from the three formal status presentation checkpoints. This dataset comprises the full audio records of the student presentations alongside the subsequent deliberative discussions among the supervisors in a private chat group. Crucially, these supervisory discussions were free-form qualitative reflections, completely decoupled from the numeric scorecasting process. To make the transcription data computationally feasible for analysis, the raw text was segmented into analytical chunks, yielding 100 distinct fragments for the 2025.2 cohort (35 at $T_1$, 37 at $T_2$, and 28 at $T_3$). M5 reconstructs one global corpus per checkpoint; chunks and source sessions are provenance units, not independent observations. Transcript coverage for 2026.1 is unavailable and is never imputed.

\textbf{Data Anonymization and Privacy:} To ensure strict ethical compliance and protect participant confidentiality, all raw data streams were subjected to a rigorous anonymization pipeline, combining deterministic heuristics with LLM-assisted redaction to scrub unstructured text across commit messages, survey free-text responses, and audio transcripts. Finalized analytical artifacts exclude direct personal identifiers. Detailed call records and raw payload provenance remain restricted to the internal reproducibility record.

\subsection{Advanced Metrics Operationalization}
\label{sec:advanced-metrics}

Sections~\ref{sec:method}A--D established what was collected. This subsection
formalizes how repository history, evaluator scorecards, survey responses, and
transcript text become nine metric families used for RQ1--RQ3. Each family
retains its analytical grain and exposes components only when they represent
distinct constructs; no component is silently combined into a new score.

\textbf{A note on $T_1$/$T_2$/$T_3$ for repository-derived measures.} The
checkpoint labels for commit and file history denote windows bounded by each
team's evaluator presentation dates, not single calendar days. This distinction
is retained throughout the repository-derived families and is made explicit in
the corresponding artifacts and provenance comments.

\subsubsection{RQ1: Generative-AI adoption and role-perception metrics}

RQ1 is operationalized at cohort-by-semester/checkpoint grain because the
anonymized survey responses contain no team identifier. M1 separates the
perception panel from self-reported usage frequency, tasks, tools, prior
experience, autonomy/tool balance, and exploratory career topics. M2 preserves
role-specific Likert distributions and paired sensitivity summaries. These
families describe self-report and perceived role disruption; neither is AI-use
telemetry or objective extinction risk.

\textbf{M1 -- Perception and self-reported usage panel.} The six perception
families remain separate. Descriptive distributions are reported alongside raw
usage fields and missingness; no heterogeneous family average is interpreted as
a behavioral dependency measure. Career-topic coding remains exploratory.

\textbf{M2 -- Perceived role disruption.} Role-specific Likert responses are
summarized by cohort, checkpoint, and role. Where privacy-preserving respondent
identifiers permit it, paired T1--T3 sensitivity summaries are reported without
exposing identities.

\subsubsection{RQ2: Repository temporal density vs. qualitative coordination friction}

\textbf{M3 -- Author activity dynamics.} M3 separates phase activity, author
concentration, and rolling seven-day activity. A secondary pooled relative-window
view removes the semester dimension only for visualization; it does not replace
semester-stratified observations.

\textbf{M4 -- Clean change dynamics.} M4 separates clean-change magnitude,
touching-commit intensity, artifact composition, and rolling clean churn. The
consistent path-classification rule defines the included source and test paths;
commit counts remain contextual rather than the primary M4 outcome.

\textbf{M5 -- Coordination evidence.} M5 reconstructs one global transcript
corpus per checkpoint and deterministically counts versioned lexical markers for
alignment, handoff, integration, blockers, and rework. It reports density,
composition, corpus coverage, and an auditable chunk queue. M5 is not joined or
correlated with team-semester M3/M4 observations.

\subsubsection{RQ3: Upfront planning quality, clean rework, and evaluator outcomes (primary)}

\textbf{M6 -- Structural and declarative planning evidence.} M6a reports T1
artifact presence and repository-visible scope. Approved M6b extraction reports
separate structured categories---goals, architecture/design, task decomposition,
risk/dependency, and literal evidence quotes---for nine observed team-semesters;
five cases without T1 commit subjects remain
\texttt{unavailable\_not\_measured}. M6b is exploratory and is never reduced
to a composite planning-quality score.

\textbf{M7 -- Repository inactivity.} M7 reports recent seven-day repository
inactivity around evaluator checkpoints and in T3-relative rolling windows. It
describes repository timing and coverage only; it does not identify planning
omission and is not an independent M9 predictor.

\textbf{M8 -- Clean rework with baseline.} M8 separates clean rework magnitude,
the clean rework ratio, the late-stage rolling trajectory, and baseline
eligibility. A path first observed at T1 or T2 is eligible for the provenance
analysis; baseline-ineligible cases remain separate and are not imputed as
stability or low quality.

\textbf{M9 -- Stratified associations.} M9 reports descriptive Spearman
associations between M6a predictors and M8a/M8b or independent T3 evaluator
outcomes, restricting ratio analyses to the baseline-eligible stratum. Approved
M6b fields enter a separate non-composite association output for observed cases.
Leave-one-out diagnostics expose influence. No M7 predictor or floor-imputation
analysis is used.

\subsection{LLM Usage in Data Extraction, Processing, and Metrics}
All LLM calls route through one centralized gateway and are persisted in the
internal structured ledger. Text tasks use \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} at temperature zero;
audio transcription uses \texttt{whisper-1}. The paper-facing methodology reports
only the role of these processes, while full prompts, payload hashes, raw
responses, costs, and cache keys remain in the internal provenance artifacts.

M1 uses coded survey responses. M5 uses deterministic lexical coding
of the transcript corpus and issues no new LLM calls. M6b uses structured
extraction from T1 commit subjects for nine observed team-semesters; five
team-semesters have no T1 subjects and remain \texttt{unavailable\_not\_measured}.
Human review approved M6b for exploratory use, with literal evidence quotes and
separate categories for goals, architecture/design, task decomposition, and
risk/dependency. M6b is not an architectural-quality score.

\textbf{Persistence and auditability.} The internal ledger records correlation
identifiers, operation/model, prompts and responses, hashes, token usage,
duration, estimated cost, and status. This supports internal tracing without
exposing the internal development history in the article narrative.

descriptive or exploratory. Missing evidence remains distinct from zero or
low quality, incompatible analytical grains are not silently combined, and no
causal claim is made without a design that supports it.
